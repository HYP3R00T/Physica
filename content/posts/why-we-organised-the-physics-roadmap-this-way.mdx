I finished my integrated master’s in physics in 2022, but I have used the subject only occasionally since then. When I started thinking about returning to it, having the degree didn’t tell me where to begin. I had studied these ideas before; I couldn’t assume that I still understood them well enough to use them. I was prepared to revisit the basics, work through derivations, and solve problems. What I needed first was some way to see the extent of the work ahead.

There was no examination to define a syllabus and no deadline to decide how much I could cover. I wanted to learn physics for the sake of understanding it. That freedom left me with decisions a course would usually make for me: which topics belong together, how deeply to study them, and what I need to understand before moving on. [Physica’s roadmap](/roadmap) grew out of trying to make those decisions visible, both for myself and for someone approaching the subject independently.

## Finding the scope of the work

The obvious starting point was a good book. We discussed John R. Taylor’s *Classical Mechanics*, and the idea of reading carefully, working through the problems, and then choosing another book. I still expect books to provide much of the teaching. The problem was using a book’s boundaries to judge the boundaries of a subject. Someone can finish an introductory treatment of mechanics with a useful understanding of motion, forces, and conservation laws while remaining unaware of the Lagrangian and Hamiltonian approaches that lie ahead. Starting with an advanced treatment creates a different difficulty: its explanations may rely on mathematics or physical ideas the reader has never learned, or no longer remembers.

I therefore wanted a view of the material that would help me choose and use those books. A list of subjects seemed like a reasonable first step. Mechanics, electromagnetism, thermodynamics, quantum mechanics: those names give the work some shape, but they leave almost every practical decision unresolved. “Learn mechanics” could mean understanding a force diagram or studying Hamiltonian dynamics. Both belong to the subject, yet they ask very different things of the learner. Before we could arrange the subjects into a route, we needed to separate which subject we were discussing from how far into it we intended to go.

That also raised the question of scope. I wanted to rebuild towards a broad master’s-level understanding and be able to recognise directions for further study. University curricula gave us reference points for what that might include, without settling every boundary for us. The roadmap still involves judgement about what belongs in the foundation, what gives useful breadth, and what introduces a more specialised framework. Those choices help explain why a topic belongs in the route, but completing it cannot amount to knowing everything in physics.

## Breaking subjects into something we can follow

We began dividing the broad subjects into segments: portions of material with a scope someone could reasonably understand and work through. Mechanics could begin with motion and Newtonian dynamics, continue through conservation laws and rotational motion, and later develop analytical and Hamiltonian methods. Keeping those segments within a recognisable branch lets the reader see both the task immediately ahead and the continuation of the subject. It also avoids treating an introductory course and an advanced course as interchangeable simply because both are called mechanics.

The size of a segment matters. If every definition becomes its own stop, the map becomes a long inventory that is difficult to read. If a whole subject occupies one stop, we are back to telling someone to “learn mechanics” without enough guidance. A segment needs enough material to develop a coherent idea, together with some indication of what understanding it would allow the learner to do. In early mechanics, that might mean being able to draw a force diagram, formulate equations of motion, and solve representative problems. Thinking about what the learner should be able to do helps us decide which ideas belong together and where a segment should end.

At first, I wanted each subject to be a continuous thread that could be followed from beginning to end. Breaking it into segments revealed why that would be awkward. A later segment may need something from another branch long before either subject is “finished”. Quantum statistical mechanics, for example, draws on both statistical mechanics and quantum ideas. Hiding that relationship inside a single subject thread would leave the learner to discover the missing preparation when the explanation stopped making sense.

The connections between segments became as important as the segments themselves. We use them to show prerequisites: the earlier material a particular segment needs. One segment can support several later directions, and several branches can meet at a topic that uses them together. This gives the roadmap its branching appearance and allows a learning order without pretending that everyone must study every subject in the same sequence. We aim to show direct dependencies, with earlier foundations reachable through those connections. Drawing every connection to every distant prerequisite would make the page harder to follow without giving the learner much additional guidance.

## Where to begin

Once we started asking what each segment required, we had to decide where to stop tracing backwards. Calculus requires familiarity with functions; functions require earlier mathematics. Continuing indefinitely would turn the roadmap into an account of someone’s entire education. I chose Class 10 as the starting point because I didn’t want to assume that a reader had taken physics and mathematics in Classes 11 and 12. The opening material therefore includes functions, trigonometry, vectors, and calculus, with room to repair specific gaps in earlier knowledge. Mathematics remains part of the physics route, introduced to support the ideas and methods that follow. We are not trying to include the whole of mathematics simply because some of it is necessary.

That starting point also helps someone returning to the subject. I don’t need to pretend that forgotten material is beneath my level, or repeat every familiar topic just because it appears earlier on the page. I can look at what a segment asks me to understand, try working with it, and revisit the parts that need attention. This makes the prerequisites useful as a guide to preparation rather than a demand to follow every row from the beginning.

## A roadmap that can grow

Choosing a starting point did not mean the rest of the route had to be settled permanently. I expect to find omissions as I study: a topic I overlooked, a connection I didn’t recognise, or a segment that needs more explanation than I anticipated. If adding those discoveries meant rebuilding the whole plan, the structure would soon get in the way of the learning. Scalability therefore became part of the design. We needed a roadmap that could accommodate more material while keeping its relationships understandable.

Suppose I realise that two existing segments need an intermediate step. I should be able to introduce it between them and revise the prerequisites to show why it belongs there. If a subject needs a deeper treatment, I can extend that branch. If another area of physics deserves its own branch, I can connect it through the ideas it draws on. These changes can happen wherever they are needed, including near the beginning of the route. The first version of the roadmap should not force every later addition to the end simply because the earlier material has already been arranged.

This flexibility matters to the reader as well. As topics are added or reorganised, the map can show them in relation to the material already there. Someone returning to it can see where the new work fits and what preparation it needs. The aim is to preserve that clarity as the amount of content grows. Adding more topics is only useful if people can still understand how to approach them.

What we have created is an organised, expandable route through physics, with subjects broken into manageable portions and connected by what they require. It gives my study a direction now, while leaving room to correct the plan as I learn more. The teaching content will grow alongside it as I write modules and notes; that work is still ahead. I want the roadmap to reflect what we discover along the way, so the next person can benefit from the gaps we found and the connections we made clearer.
