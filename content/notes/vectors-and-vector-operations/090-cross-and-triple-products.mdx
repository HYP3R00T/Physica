## Cross product

<Definition title="Cross product in three dimensions">

In a right-handed orthonormal coordinate system, for $\mathbf u,\mathbf v\in\mathbb R^3$,

$$
\mathbf u\times\mathbf v
=(u_2v_3-u_3v_2,\ u_3v_1-u_1v_3,\ u_1v_2-u_2v_1)
$$

</Definition>

The result is perpendicular to both inputs. For nonparallel, nonzero vectors, its direction follows the right-hand rule from $\mathbf u$ toward $\mathbf v$, and

$$
\|\mathbf u\times\mathbf v\|=\|\mathbf u\|\,\|\mathbf v\|\sin\theta
$$

This magnitude is the area of their parallelogram; half of it is the area of their triangle. The cross product is zero exactly when the inputs are dependent. For example, $\mathbf e_1\times\mathbf e_2=\mathbf e_3$.

The cross product is distributive and scales linearly in each input, but reversing the inputs changes its sign:

$$
\mathbf u\times\mathbf v=-\mathbf v\times\mathbf u
$$

It is not associative. For example, $(\mathbf e_1\times\mathbf e_1)\times\mathbf e_2=\mathbf0$, whereas $\mathbf e_1\times(\mathbf e_1\times\mathbf e_2)=-\mathbf e_2$. This three-dimensional product is not an operation required by the vector-space axioms.

## Scalar triple product

The scalar triple product is

$$
\mathbf u\cdot(\mathbf v\times\mathbf w)
$$

It gives signed volume; its absolute value is the volume of the parallelepiped generated by the three vectors. Cyclically rotating the inputs preserves its value, and exchanging two inputs reverses the sign. It is zero exactly when the three vectors are dependent, so their displacements lie in a common plane through the origin.

For four points $A,B,C,D$ in three dimensions, test coplanarity by applying the scalar triple product to $B-A$, $C-A$ and $D-A$.

## Vector triple products

Parentheses matter. The identities are

$$
\mathbf u\times(\mathbf v\times\mathbf w)
=\mathbf v(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf w)-\mathbf w(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v),
$$

$$
(\mathbf u\times\mathbf v)\times\mathbf w
=\mathbf v(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf w)-\mathbf u(\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf w)
$$

These convert nested cross products into linear combinations and dot products. A nonzero $\mathbf u\times\mathbf v$ also supplies a normal for the plane spanned by two independent directions $\mathbf u,\mathbf v$.
